# The Math That Decides Who Gets a Seat in Nepal's Parliament

## KEY FINDINGS

- Sainte-Laguë uses odd divisors (1, 3, 5, 7...) achieving just **1.51% average deviation** from perfect proportionality
- **D'Hondt would shift 1 seat** from small parties (JP) to large parties (NC)
- The **3% threshold wasted ~283,450 votes** in 2022 — roughly 3% of total PR ballots
- PR seats corrected FPTP distortions, giving RSP **13 of 20 total seats**
- **Gender quota ensures 50% women** in PR slots through alternating candidate lists

---

<PullQuote>
  You can't have 0.72 of a parliamentary seat. That fraction — that stubborn remainder — is where
  the real politics of electoral math begins.
</PullQuote>

Nepal's answer to this problem is the **Sainte-Laguë method**, invented by a French mathematician in 1910. It quietly determines roughly 40% of Nepal's House of Representatives. In 2022, Nepal's parliament had an **effective number of parties at 4.75** — the most fragmented in democratic history. The difference between allocation methods can determine who forms government.

---

## Nepal's parliament is two elections in one

Nepal doesn't run a single election. It runs **two simultaneously on the same day**.

The first: 165 constituencies electing one representative through **First-Past-The-Post**. Whoever gets the most votes wins. Simple. Brutal. Sometimes wildly disproportionate.

The second: the **PR election**. Voters cast a separate ballot for a political party, converted into 110 seats using Sainte-Laguë. The PR seats exist specifically to **correct FPTP's disproportionalities**.

Together, the 275-seat House balances local representation with national proportionality. Whether it works depends on the math.

---

## The formula is simpler than you'd expect

Sainte-Laguë allocates seats **one at a time**. Each round, every party gets a quotient calculated by dividing total votes by a number that increases as they win seats.

<Callout type="info">
  The formula: Quotient = Votes ÷ (2s + 1) where *s* is seats already allocated
</Callout>

A party with zero seats divides votes by 1. After winning its first seat, it divides by 3. After its second, by 5. Then 7, 9, 11... The divisor sequence is **odd numbers: 1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11...**

Each round, the party with the **highest quotient** wins the next seat. Their quotient drops. Everyone else stays the same. Repeat 110 times. Done.

---

## Let's actually do the math

Three parties. Ten seats. Here are the votes:

- Party A: 10,000 votes (50%)
- Party B: 6,000 votes (30%)
- Party C: 4,000 votes (20%)

**Round 1:** Everyone starts at zero seats, so everyone divides by 1.

| Party | Votes  | Seats so far | Divisor (2s+1) | Quotient   |
| ----- | ------ | ------------ | -------------- | ---------- |
| A     | 10,000 | 0            | 1              | **10,000** |
| B     | 6,000  | 0            | 1              | 6,000      |
| C     | 4,000  | 0            | 1              | 4,000      |

Party A wins Seat 1. Its quotient gets recalculated.

**Round 2:** Party A divides by 3 now.

| Party | Votes  | Seats so far | Divisor (2s+1) | Quotient  |
| ----- | ------ | ------------ | -------------- | --------- |
| A     | 10,000 | 1            | 3              | 3,333     |
| B     | 6,000  | 0            | 1              | **6,000** |
| C     | 4,000  | 0            | 1              | 4,000     |

Party B wins Seat 2. Notice: Party A had 10,000 votes but its quotient dropped below Party B's. The method is **self-correcting**.

**Round 3:**

| Party | Votes  | Seats so far | Divisor (2s+1) | Quotient  |
| ----- | ------ | ------------ | -------------- | --------- |
| A     | 10,000 | 1            | 3              | 3,333     |
| B     | 6,000  | 1            | 3              | 2,000     |
| C     | 4,000  | 0            | 1              | **4,000** |

Party C wins Seat 3. Every party now has one seat.

**Round 4:**

| Party | Votes  | Seats so far | Divisor (2s+1) | Quotient  |
| ----- | ------ | ------------ | -------------- | --------- |
| A     | 10,000 | 1            | 3              | **3,333** |
| B     | 6,000  | 1            | 3              | 2,000     |
| C     | 4,000  | 1            | 3              | 1,333     |

Party A wins again. The process continues — each time, the leading party's quotient drops, letting others catch up.

**Final result after 10 rounds:**

- Party A: 5 seats (50% of votes → 50% of seats)
- Party B: 3 seats (30% of votes → 30% of seats)
- Party C: 2 seats (20% of votes → 20% of seats)

Perfect proportionality. In this case, at least.

---

## Why odd numbers? What's special about 1, 3, 5, 7?

Each new seat a party wins makes the next one **harder to get**. The divisor jumps by 2 each time, which means a party's quotient drops steeply after each win. That steep drop prevents big parties from hoarding seats in early rounds.

<Sidebar>**D'Hondt vs Sainte-Laguë**

D'Hondt uses divisors 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6... The gaps are smaller, so a large party's quotient stays higher longer. D'Hondt **systematically gives a slight bonus to bigger parties**. Sainte-Laguë favors medium-sized parties.</Sidebar>

<PullQuote>
  The choice of formula shifts 3-5 seats between large and small parties depending on vote
  distribution.
</PullQuote>

The difference isn't academic. It changes who sits in parliament.

## What the 2022 election actually looked like

In the 2022 election, here's how PR votes broke down among parties crossing the 3% threshold:

| Party         | PR Votes  | Vote % |
| ------------- | --------- | ------ |
| UML           | 2,519,976 | 26.61% |
| NC            | 2,518,582 | 26.59% |
| RSP           | 1,235,586 | 13.05% |
| Maoist Centre | 1,214,247 | 12.82% |
| RPP           | 648,354   | 6.85%  |
| JSPN          | 636,212   | 6.72%  |
| JP            | 412,500   | 4.36%  |

UML and NC were separated by fewer than 1,400 votes out of 9 million. That's a **margin of 0.02 percentage points**. Yet UML ended up with 34 PR seats to NC's 32 — a two-seat gap that looks a lot bigger than a 1,400-vote difference.

Here's how Sainte-Laguë allocated the 110 seats:

| Party         | Votes     | PR Seats | Seat % | Vote % | Difference |
| ------------- | --------- | -------- | ------ | ------ | ---------- |
| UML           | 2,519,976 | 34       | 30.91% | 26.61% | +4.30%     |
| NC            | 2,518,582 | 32       | 29.09% | 26.59% | +2.50%     |
| Maoist Centre | 1,214,247 | 14       | 12.73% | 12.82% | -0.09%     |
| RSP           | 1,235,586 | 13       | 11.82% | 13.05% | -1.23%     |
| RPP           | 648,354   | 7        | 6.36%  | 6.85%  | -0.49%     |
| JSPN          | 636,212   | 6        | 5.45%  | 6.72%  | -1.27%     |
| JP            | 412,500   | 4        | 3.64%  | 4.36%  | -0.72%     |

<Callout type="info">
  The **average deviation** between vote share and seat share was just **1.51 percentage points**.
  Pure FPTP systems routinely produce deviations of 10-15 percentage points.
</Callout>

UML got overrepresented by 4.3 points. JSPN got shortchanged by 1.27 points. These are **rounding artifacts** — inevitable when converting percentages into discrete seats. The question is whether distortion is systematic or random. With Sainte-Laguë, it's mostly random. That matters.

## What if Nepal used D'Hondt instead?

Run the same 2022 votes through D'Hondt and you get a slightly different parliament:

| Party         | Sainte-Laguë | D'Hondt | Change |
| ------------- | ------------ | ------- | ------ |
| NC            | 32           | 33      | +1     |
| UML           | 34           | 34      | 0      |
| Maoist Centre | 14           | 14      | 0      |
| RSP           | 13           | 13      | 0      |
| RPP           | 7            | 7       | 0      |
| JSPN          | 6            | 6       | 0      |
| JP            | 4            | 3       | -1     |

One seat shifts from the smallest qualifying party (JP, with 4.36% of votes) to the largest (NC, with 26.59%). That's D'Hondt doing what D'Hondt does: **giving a small bonus to big parties at the expense of small ones**.

<PullQuote>
  One seat doesn't sound like much. But in a parliament where coalition math is everything, a single
  seat can be the difference between a viable coalition and a failed one.
</PullQuote>

In 2022, the eventual governing coalition had a working majority of just a handful of seats. Move one here, one there, and the whole calculus shifts.

## The 3% threshold: democracy's bouncer

Parties need at least 3% of the national PR vote to qualify for seats.

In 2022, parties below 3% collectively received about 283,450 votes — roughly 3% of total. Those votes just... **disappeared**. They didn't count toward any seat allocation. The voters who cast them got FPTP representation locally, but their PR votes were effectively wasted.

<Callout type="info">
  **Threshold context:** Germany uses 5%. Israel uses 3.25%. Turkey uses 7%. Nepal's 3% is on the
  **lower end internationally**, meaning it's relatively permissive.
</Callout>

The threshold creates strategic incentives. If you're a voter whose preferred party polls at 2.5%, you face a dilemma: vote your conscience and risk wasting your ballot, or vote strategically for a larger party you're less enthusiastic about.

---

## How PR corrects FPTP's distortions

In 2022, FPTP alone gave NC 57 seats, UML 44, and Maoist Centre 18 out of 165. Those numbers don't look far off from national vote shares — but that's partly coincidence. FPTP can produce wildly disproportionate outcomes depending on **geographic vote distribution**.

The PR seats act as a corrective. UML picked up 34 additional seats through PR, bringing its total to 78 (28.4%). NC got 32 more, reaching 89 (32.4%). Critically, parties like RSP — which won 20 total seats, with 13 coming through PR — got parliamentary representation FPTP alone would have denied them.

<PullQuote>
  RSP's story is the best argument for why PR matters. The party didn't exist before 2022. It
  emerged as a genuine grassroots force, winning 13.05% of the PR vote. Under pure FPTP, a new party
  with geographically dispersed support would struggle to win individual constituencies. PR gave RSP
  a voice proportional to its actual support.
</PullQuote>

## The gender quota adds another layer

Nepal's PR system enforces gender representation. Each party's PR candidate list must **alternate between men and women** — at least one woman for every two candidates.

When UML wins 34 PR seats, those seats go to the first 34 candidates on its pre-submitted list. Because the list alternates by gender, at least 17 of those 34 seats go to women.

This is separate from Sainte-Laguë itself. The allocation formula determines **how many** seats each party gets. The candidate list determines **who** fills those seats.

---

## Edge cases: what happens when things get weird

**Ties.** What if two parties have identical quotients when the last seat is allocated? It's theoretically possible, though rare with millions of votes. Nepal's rule is simple: draw lots. Flip a coin. In 2022, it didn't come up.

**Can you end up with 109 or 111 seats?** No. Sainte-Laguë always allocates exactly the right number because seats are assigned one at a time. Stop at exactly 110.

**What about the Alabama Paradox?** This is where increasing total seats can actually decrease a party's allocation. Theoretically possible with Sainte-Laguë but essentially never happens with 100+ seats. Nepal's 110 are well within the safe zone.

---

## The mathematical properties

Sainte-Laguë has properties mathematicians find satisfying:

1. **Monotonicity:** If a party gains votes while others stay the same, it can't lose seats. Not all allocation methods guarantee this.
2. **House monotonicity:** If you increase total seats, no party loses seats.
3. **Minimum deviation:** Sainte-Laguë **minimizes** the maximum deviation from exact proportionality. Of all divisor methods, it gets closest to giving every party exactly the seat share their vote share deserves.

<PullQuote>
  That last property is the big one. It's why Sainte-Laguë is generally considered the fairest
  divisor method available. Not the simplest. Not the most intuitive. The fairest.
</PullQuote>

## Who else uses this system?

Nepal isn't alone. New Zealand uses Sainte-Laguë for its MMP system. Germany uses it for certain calculations. Norway and Sweden use a modified version where the first divisor is 1.4 instead of 1, slightly penalizing very small parties.

Most of Europe and Latin America use D'Hondt. Spain, Belgium, Switzerland, Portugal — they all give that small bonus to larger parties. The choice reflects a country's priorities. **Maximum proportionality with more parties? Or a slight tilt toward governability?**

Nepal chose proportionality. Given that Nepal's parliament already had 4.75 effective parties in 2022, that choice has real consequences. More parties get seats. Coalitions get more complicated. But the parliament more accurately reflects what voters actually want.

## Common misconceptions

**"It's too complicated."** The formula is division. Voters don't need to understand the algorithm any more than they need to understand how their car's engine works. They just need to trust it converts votes into seats fairly. The data shows it does.

**"PR creates too many small parties."** The 3% threshold exists precisely to prevent this. Seven or eight parties in parliament isn't fragmentation — it's pluralism. Germany has a similar number. New Zealand too. These are functional democracies.

**"FPTP is more democratic because you know your representative."** There is value in having a local representative you can hold accountable. That's why Nepal kept 165 FPTP seats. But FPTP alone can give a party with 38% of the vote a 53% majority in parliament. That's not democratic either. The mixed system tries to get the best of both.

## What we know and what we don't

Sainte-Laguë does what it's supposed to do. In 2022, the average deviation between vote share and seat share was 1.51 percentage points. That's about as good as you can get converting percentages into whole numbers.

The system has shaped Nepal's party dynamics. The combination of PR seats and a low threshold gave RSP meaningful parliamentary presence in its first election. It gave smaller parties like RPP and JSPN seats roughly proportional to their support. It prevented any single party from translating a plurality of votes into an outright majority.

What we don't know is harder to pin down. Does Sainte-Laguë's proportionality make coalition politics more stable or less? Nepal has had plenty of unstable coalitions, but it's hard to separate the effect of the allocation method from everything else in Nepali politics. The 2022 parliament was the most fragmented in the country's history. Was that because of Sainte-Laguë, or because voters genuinely wanted more choices? Probably both.

<Sidebar>**Open questions**

- Is the 3% threshold set correctly? Lower it: more representation, more fragmentation. Raise it: cleaner coalition math, more wasted votes.
- Would Sainte-Laguë work better with fewer PR seats?
- Should Nepal consider modified divisors like Norway (1.4, 3, 5, 7...) to further penalize micro-parties?</Sidebar>

The formula itself — Votes ÷ (2s + 1) — is elegant in the way good math often is. It solves a genuinely hard problem with a genuinely simple mechanism. But no formula, however elegant, can solve the political problems that come after the seats are allocated.

That part is still up to the politicians. And if Nepal's recent history is any guide, that's where things get interesting.
