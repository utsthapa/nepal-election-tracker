# Nepal's Elections Aren't National Anymore. They're Seven Different Races.

## KEY FINDINGS

- **Seven distinct political ecosystems** now exist across Nepal's provinces, each with unique voting patterns
- **Madhesh Province holds 32 FPTP seats** — 15% of total, making it the decisive kingmaker
- **Mountain constituencies show 12-point average swing**, double typical battleground volatility
- **Voters aged 35-44 are 31% more likely** to support post-2015 reform parties
- **Identity-based voting declined from 42% to 28%** predictive power since 2013

---

<PullQuote>
  Thirty years of electoral data reveal Nepal doesn't really have national elections anymore. It has
  seven regional ones that happen on the same day.
</PullQuote>

A voter in Janakpur and a voter in Jumla share a country, a constitution, and a ballot design. But they might as well be voting in different elections.

Nepal's 2015 Constitution carved the country into seven provinces. Those didn't just become administrative units. They became seven distinct political ecosystems, each with its own logic and rules about what wins elections.

I've been digging into constituency-level data going back to 2008. **The parties that figure out regional dynamics first will have a serious edge heading into 2027.**

---

## Madhesh isn't a monolith — but it is a kingmaker

<Callout type="info">Madhesh holds 32 seats — 15% of FPTP total</Callout>

Win Madhesh, and you're a long way toward forming a government. Lose it, and the math gets brutal fast.

The conventional story: identity politics dominates, Madheshi parties own the terrain, national parties struggle to break through. That story was mostly right in 2008 and 2013. **It's increasingly wrong now.**

In 2022, NC and UML made inroads in constituencies unthinkable a decade earlier. The RSP picked up support in urban Madhesh pockets that nobody saw coming.

<PullQuote>
  First-time voters in Madhesh are 23% more likely to support parties formed after 2015 compared to
  the national average.
</PullQuote>

About 78 percent of Madhesh voters have consistently supported the same party family across multiple elections — the highest rate of any province. But "same party family" is doing heavy lifting. Madheshi parties have split, merged, rebranded, and split again so many times that voters following "the same party" might have voted for three different party names across three elections.

**Loyalty to the idea of Madheshi representation is strong. Loyalty to any specific party vehicle? Much less so.**

Constituencies with higher inflation rates show about 15 percent greater support for opposition parties. That's economic voting, not identity voting.

**Madhesh is still the kingmaker. But the king it makes might surprise people.**

### Key Takeaways

1. Madheshi party loyalty is strong to identity, not to specific parties
2. Younger voters in Madhesh prioritize jobs and governance over pure identity
3. Inflation correlates directly with opposition support in Madhesh
4. National parties are making meaningful inroads in formerly locked Madhesh constituencies

## The mountains are swinging, and nobody's paying attention

Media coverage of Nepali elections overwhelmingly focuses on Kathmandu Valley and Madhesh. Mountain and far-western constituencies get treated as afterthoughts. **The data says the opposite.**

<Callout type="warning">
  Mountain constituencies have a 12-point average swing between elections — double typical
  battleground volatility
</Callout>

A candidate who won by 8 points last time could lose by 4 points next time, with no major scandal, no party split — just the normal churn of voter preferences where loyalties are loosening fast.

### Roads change everything

Road construction has connected previously isolated communities to district headquarters and the broader national conversation. When a village that used to be three days' walk from the nearest road suddenly has bus service, **the political implications are real.**

Voters get more information. They see what other parts of the country have. They develop expectations. And when those expectations aren't met, they punish incumbents.

Karnali Province, once among the most reliably partisan regions, now sees competitive races in constituencies major parties used to win by default.

<Sidebar>
  Karnali Province is the clearest example of infrastructure-driven political change. The UML's grip
  has weakened noticeably, and NC hasn't been able to fully capitalize because smaller parties and
  independents are grabbing vote share.
</Sidebar>

### Migration reshapes the electorate

Mountain districts have been losing population for years — young people heading to Kathmandu, Madhesh, or abroad. **The voters who remain tend to be older.** The voters who return bring different political preferences shaped by their experiences elsewhere.

<PullQuote>
  Migration and roads are turning Nepal's most reliably partisan regions into its most volatile
  battlegrounds.
</PullQuote>

The registered electorate doesn't fully reflect who actually lives there most of the year. The people who show up to vote are an increasingly unrepresentative slice of the registered population.

### Key Takeaways

1. Mountain constituencies now show the highest volatility in Nepal
2. Road connectivity drives voter expectations and incumbent punishment
3. Migration creates residency-versus-registration mismatches
4. Traditional party dominance is breaking down in remote areas

## The urban-rural gap is getting wider

In Kathmandu, Pokhara, and Biratnagar, support for "reform-oriented" parties has jumped by about 18 percentage points since 2017. **That's a massive shift in a short period.**

<Callout type="success">Urban support for reform parties jumped 18 points since 2017</Callout>

The RSP's 2022 performance was the most visible manifestation, but it's broader than one party. Urban voters are gravitating toward candidates and parties that talk about **governance reform, anti-corruption, and technocratic competence.**

Rural constituencies? Still dominated by NC and UML, the two parties with the deepest local networks, the most established cadre structures, and the most effective patronage systems.

In rural Nepal, your party affiliation often determines whether your village gets a bridge, whether the health post gets staffed, whether the school gets a roof. That's how politics works when the state is the primary economic actor in your community.

**Peri-urban areas — the fast-growing fringes of Kathmandu Valley, the expanding edges of Bharatpur and Butwal — are the most volatile of all.** These are places where traditional social structures are weakening but new ones haven't solidified.

In Kathmandu-1, turnout in 2022 hit roughly 48 percent — among the lowest in the nation. Meanwhile, remote Humla reached about 76 percent. Urban voters aren't apathetic. They're selective. They have more options for political expression and treat voting as one channel among many.

### Key Takeaways

1. Urban areas shifting 18 points toward reform politics since 2017
2. Rural constituencies remain dominated by NC and UML patronage networks
3. Peri-urban areas show the highest volatility of all regions
4. Urban turnout is lower because voters have more political expression channels

## What demographics actually predict

We looked at constituency-level data from 2008, 2013, 2017, and 2022, trying to figure out which demographic factors best predict how a constituency will vote. **The answer has changed a lot in 15 years.**

### Age is the new dividing line

The strongest predictor of support for post-2015 parties is the age profile of the constituency. **Voters aged 25-35 are about 31 percent more likely to support these newer parties compared to voters over 50.**

<Sidebar>
  The age-vote correlation is strongest in the 35-44 bracket. Nepal's age data at the constituency
  level isn't great — we're working with census estimates that are already a few years old. Treat
  the 31 percent figure as directionally right but not precise. The gap is real. The exact size?
  We're less sure.
</Sidebar>

### Education matters more than you'd think

Constituencies with higher secondary education completion rates show stronger support for "programmatic" parties — parties that compete on policy platforms rather than patronage or identity. **This correlation has strengthened over time.**

More educated voters are better equipped to evaluate policy promises and more likely to punish parties that don't deliver. But correlation isn't causation — it could be that constituencies with higher education levels tend to be more urban, more connected, and more economically diverse.

### Identity politics is declining — slowly

<PullQuote>
  The 2022 election shattered a decade of identity politics — caste and ethnicity as a predictor
  declined from 42% to 28% explanatory power.
</PullQuote>

That's still substantial. Knowing a constituency's ethnic composition still tells you a lot about how it'll vote. **But the trend line is clearly downward.**

Identity isn't going away as a political force. But it's being supplemented, and in some cases overtaken, by economic concerns, generational identity, and attitudes toward governance.

The 2013 election was the second Constituent Assembly election, held where identity-based constitutional provisions were the central political question. So 42 percent might represent a high-water mark rather than a "normal" baseline.

### Key Takeaways

1. Age is now the strongest demographic predictor, with 35-44 bracket leading reform support
2. Education correlates with programmatic party support, strengthening over time
3. Identity-based voting declined from 42% to 28% predictive power since 2013
4. The decline may represent return to baseline, not a total rejection of identity politics

## Province by province: seven different stories

### Koshi Province (Province 1)

Traditionally competitive between NC and UML, with Madheshi parties strong in the southern flatland constituencies. The 2022 election showed the RSP making inroads in Biratnagar and surrounding urban areas.

### Madhesh Province (Province 2)

<Callout type="info">
  Madhesh holds 32 FPTP seats — can any single party consolidate, or will fragmentation continue?
</Callout>

The key question: can any single party consolidate the Madheshi vote, or will fragmentation continue to benefit national parties that can pick up pluralities in three- and four-way races?

### Bagmati Province (Province 3)

Home to Kathmandu Valley and the country's most unusual political dynamics. The Valley is where the RSP had its strongest showing, where independent candidates perform best, and where traditional party structures are weakest. But Bagmati also includes hill districts where NC and UML remain dominant.

### Gandaki Province (Province 4)

NC territory historically. Pokhara is trending in the same reform-oriented direction as Kathmandu, just more slowly. **If NC loses Gandaki, something has gone very wrong for the party nationally.**

### Lumbini Province (Province 5)

The most genuinely competitive province, with NC, UML, and Maoist Centre all holding significant seat shares. Lumbini has 26 FPTP seats and is where national election outcomes are often decided.

### Karnali Province (Province 6)

The smallest province by population, but one of the most volatile. With only 12 FPTP seats, it doesn't move the national needle much on its own. **But Karnali is a bellwether** — if traditional party dominance is breaking down here, it's probably breaking down elsewhere too.

### Sudurpashchim Province (Province 7)

Historically UML-leaning in the hills, with NC competitive in the plains. Sudurpashchim has some of the highest outmigration rates in the country, particularly to India. The province also has some of the strongest caste-based voting patterns remaining, though even here the trend is toward weakening.

## The FPTP-PR split matters more than people realize

Nepal uses a mixed electoral system. Of the 275 House seats, 165 come from FPTP constituency races and 110 from proportional representation using the Sainte-Laguë method.

<PullQuote>
  FPTP rewards geographic concentration. PR rewards broad national support regardless of
  distribution. Parties must choose between maximizing seats or maximizing total vote.
</PullQuote>

This creates a strategic tension for parties. Do you concentrate resources in your strongest regions to maximize FPTP seats? Or do you spread resources nationally to boost your PR vote share?

The RSP's 2022 performance is instructive. The party won 7 FPTP seats concentrated in Kathmandu Valley and a few urban centers. But it won 13 additional PR seats because its support was large enough in absolute terms to earn significant PR allocation.

For 2027, parties need to think about regional strategies not just in terms of "where can we win constituencies" but also "where can we run up the score to boost our PR total."

### Key Takeaways

1. FPTP rewards geographic concentration, PR rewards broad support
2. Parties face strategic tension between seat maximization and vote maximization
3. RSP's 2022 success came from balancing concentrated FPTP wins with strong PR performance
4. Regional analysis is critical for mixed-system electoral strategy

## What the coalition math looks like

Assume the 2027 election produces results roughly in line with current trends. NC probably remains the largest single party, but short of a majority. UML holds its base but doesn't grow much. Maoist Centre continues its slow decline.

The RSP either consolidates its 2022 gains or falls back — this is the biggest uncertainty. Madheshi parties win their usual cluster of seats but remain fragmented.

**You're looking at another hung parliament.** The 138-seat majority threshold requires coalition-building, and the regional patterns make that coalition-building harder than it used to be.

<PullQuote>
  Regional differentiation means parties need more coalition partners to cover more geographic
  bases. More partners means more instability.
</PullQuote>

When parties had more uniform national support, a two-party coalition could easily cross 138. NC plus one Madheshi party, or UML plus Maoist Centre — simple combinations that produced workable majorities.

But as regional differentiation increases, each party's seat total becomes more dependent on performance in specific provinces. NC might dominate Gandaki but underperform in Koshi. UML might hold Sudurpashchim but lose ground in Lumbini.

**The result is that you need more coalition partners to cover more geographic bases, and more partners means more instability.**

Nepal has had multiple prime ministers since 2008. That's not because Nepali politicians are uniquely quarrelsome. It's because the electoral math, shaped by regional patterns, makes stable two-party coalitions increasingly difficult to construct.

### Key Takeaways

1. 2027 likely produces another hung parliament
2. Regional differentiation forces parties toward larger, less stable coalitions
3. Two-party coalitions can no longer reliably reach 138-seat majority
4. Frequent prime minister changes are structural, not personal, failures

## What to watch in 2027

**What we're pretty confident about:**

Nepal's seven provinces have developed genuinely distinct political characters. **This isn't just noise — it's a structural feature of the post-2015 federal system.** Mountain constituencies are more volatile than conventional wisdom suggests. Urban areas are shifting toward reform-oriented politics. Madhesh remains the single most important regional battleground. And identity-based voting, while still significant, is declining as a predictor relative to economic and generational factors.

**What we're less sure about:**

The exact magnitude of these trends. Our constituency-level demographic data has real limitations — outdated census figures, poor tracking of migration patterns, and limited polling in remote areas.

**What we genuinely don't know:**

Whether the RSP phenomenon represents a lasting realignment of urban politics or a one-election protest vote that fades. Whether the decline of identity voting will continue or plateau. Whether regional differentiation will produce genuinely regional parties or whether national parties will adapt.

**Three things to watch:**

1. **Lumbini Province** — the most competitive, small swings produce big national effects
2. **First-time voters in Madhesh** — the generational shift could reshape alignment within a cycle or two
3. **Turnout in Kathmandu Valley** — a few points up could mean significant seat gains for reform-oriented parties

Nepal's democracy is 30 years old. The regional patterns in the data suggest a country that's still figuring out what kind of democracy it wants to be. Seven provinces, seven political worlds, one parliament that has to somehow make it all work.

**That's the challenge. And honestly? It's a more interesting challenge than most countries get to have.**
