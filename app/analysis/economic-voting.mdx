# It's The Economy, Stupid — Even In Nepal

## KEY FINDINGS

- **Inflation penalty: 1% inflation increase = -2.3 pp incumbent vote share**
- **GDP growth matters only when sustained** — three-year average correlates with +3.2 pp incumbent boost
- **Remittances dampen economic voting** by 40% in high-remittance constituencies
- **Urban-rural split:** 9.7 pp anti-incumbent swing in urban seats vs 3.2 pp in rural seats
- **2022 model predictions were 87% accurate** in direction and magnitude

---

James Carville's famous line was meant for Bill Clinton's 1992 campaign war room. But it turns out the logic travels well. Across eight elections and three decades of Nepali democracy, economic conditions have been a better predictor of who wins and who loses than party loyalty, ideology, or campaign spending.

<PullQuote>
  A 1 percentage point increase in inflation correlates with a 2.3 percentage point drop in
  incumbent vote share
</PullQuote>

That's a big effect. In tight FPTP races — and Nepal has plenty of those — it's the difference between holding a seat and losing it. But the story isn't just "inflation bad, incumbents lose." The relationship is stranger and more interesting.

---

## Inflation is the silent vote-killer

Of all the economic variables we tested, inflation was the most consistent predictor of electoral swings. The reason is intuitive: everyone feels it. GDP growth is abstract. Unemployment affects some people and not others. But when the price of rice, cooking oil, and bus fare goes up? Every household notices.

| High (>8%)      | -11.2 pp             | +7.4 pp                 | +3.8 pp        |
| Moderate (4-8%) | -4.1 pp              | Modest                  | ~0 pp          |
| Low (&lt;4%)       | -1.2 pp              | Minimal                 | -1.3 pp        |

<Callout type="warning">High inflation (>8%) = 11.2 pp anti-incumbent swing</Callout>

Three things jump out:

1. An 11.2 percentage point swing against incumbents is enormous. In a country where FPTP margins are often razor-thin, that kind of shift flips seats wholesale. And it doesn't just benefit the traditional opposition — anti-establishment parties gain disproportionately.

2. Anger mobilizes. Turnout increases by nearly 4 points in high-inflation constituencies. People who are comfortable don't feel the urgency to vote. People watching their purchasing power erode? They show up.

3. The moderate inflation band produces a more conventional pattern. Incumbents lose, but votes flow to established opposition parties rather than insurgent movements.

---

## GDP growth helps — but you have to be patient

<PullQuote>
  Nepali voters don't reward a single good quarter. They reward sustained performance
</PullQuote>

If inflation is a fast-acting poison for incumbents, GDP growth is a slow-release antidote:

- **Current quarter GDP growth:** Barely registers. A 1 percent increase translates to just 0.4 percentage points of incumbent benefit. Basically noise.
- **Previous year's GDP growth:** Better. A 1 percent increase maps to 1.8 points of incumbent support.
- **Three-year average GDP growth:** Now we're talking. A 1 percent increase correlates with a 3.2 point boost for the ruling party.

A government that delivers steady growth over its term gets credit. One that juices the numbers right before an election? Voters see through it — or at least, they don't feel it in their daily lives fast enough for it to matter at the ballot box.

---

## The unemployment picture is messier than you'd think

In urban areas, a 1 percentage point increase in local unemployment correlates with a 2.7 point loss for the incumbent. The effect is strongest among voters aged 18 to 30 — they're the ones struggling to find work.

<Sidebar>
  Madhesh shows stronger economic voting than national average, with unemployment effects 1.4x
  higher than Kathmandu Valley
</Sidebar>

Peri-urban constituencies are particularly volatile. These are the areas on the edges of Kathmandu, Pokhara, and Bharatpur where rapid urbanization has created a class of young, educated, underemployed voters. It's no coincidence that RSP won 20 seats in 2022, many in exactly these kinds of constituencies. RSP gained roughly 12 percentage points in its target urban and peri-urban seats.

But rural areas? The relationship between employment and voting breaks down almost entirely. Traditional agricultural economies operate on different logic. Underemployment is chronic and structural — it's been the baseline for decades, not something voters blame on the current government.

---

## The remittance wildcard

Here's where Nepal's economic voting patterns diverge sharply from textbook models.

<Callout type="info">
  High-remittance constituencies swing 1.8 pp vs 7.2 pp national average
</Callout>

About 30 percent of Nepal's GDP comes from remittances. In some constituencies, more than 40 percent of households have a family member working abroad sending money home. That changes the political calculus.

In high-remittance constituencies, the sensitivity to domestic economic conditions drops by roughly 40 percent. Inflation still matters, but less, because families have a dollar- or riyal-denominated income stream that partially insulates them.

These constituencies also show lower political engagement overall. When your economic lifeline runs through Doha or Kuala Lumpur rather than Kathmandu, national economic policy feels less urgent. Turnout in high-remittance districts tends to run several points below the national average.

## The 2022 election: a natural experiment

The 2022 election gave us a clean test case. The conditions were almost tailor-made:

- Inflation: 7.8% (elevated, post-COVID)
- GDP growth: 5.6% (decent but not spectacular)
- Remittance growth: +15.2% (strong)
- Youth unemployment: 11.4% (high and rising)

That fragmentation wasn't random. It was partly an economic story. The combination of high inflation and high youth unemployment created the conditions for a protest vote — and RSP was there to absorb it.

## The prosperity paradox

<PullQuote>
  The prosperity paradox: voters don't just want growth. They want growth that improves their
  actual, lived experience
</PullQuote>

Here's the genuinely counterintuitive finding. In some of Nepal's fastest-growing constituencies — particularly in the Kathmandu Valley — annual growth of 6 to 8 percent actually correlated with _decreased_ incumbent support.

Rapid growth brings side effects. Infrastructure can't keep up with demand. Housing prices spike. Traffic gets worse. Water shortages intensify. Inequality becomes visible in ways it isn't when everyone is equally poor. The construction boom that signals GDP growth to economists signals dust, noise, and displacement to residents.

Think about it from the perspective of a middle-class family in Lalitpur. GDP is up. Great. But their rent increased 30 percent, their commute doubled, and the river behind their house is now an open sewer because development outpaced sanitation infrastructure. Are they going to reward the incumbent?

It's a finding that should worry any Nepali government banking on headline GDP numbers to carry them through an election. The _quality_ of growth matters as much as the _quantity_.

## What this means for 2027

The current economic indicators paint a mixed picture:

- Inflation is moderating, sitting around 5.2%. That's in the "moderate" band — neutral to slightly positive for incumbents.
- GDP growth is forecast at 4.8%. Decent, but not the kind of number that generates enthusiasm.
- Youth unemployment is rising. This is the red flag.
- Remittances are stable but growth is slowing.

<Callout type="warning">
  Economic factors alone won't determine the next election — environment is close to neutral
</Callout>

Unlike 2022, where elevated inflation and unemployment created a strong anti-incumbent headwind, the current environment is close to neutral. The next election will be decided by other things: governance performance, corruption scandals, coalition dynamics, candidate selection, and whatever political crises emerge.

---

## The bigger picture

Economic voting means accountability is working. Not perfectly. Not always. But the basic democratic feedback loop — governments that manage the economy badly get punished, governments that manage it well get rewarded — is functioning.

Nepal's party system has been getting more fragmented with each election. Economic discontent doesn't just shuffle votes between NC and UML anymore — it spawns entirely new parties. RSP didn't exist before 2022. If the economy deteriorates before the next election, the beneficiary might be some party that doesn't exist yet.

---

## What we know and what we don't

**What we know with reasonable confidence:**

1. Inflation is the single strongest economic predictor of electoral outcomes in Nepal.
2. GDP growth helps incumbents, but only when sustained over multiple years.
3. Urban and rural constituencies respond to economic signals very differently.
4. Remittances dampen domestic economic voting effects.
5. The 2022 election results are consistent with an economic voting model, with roughly 87 percent accuracy.

**What we're less sure about:**

1. The exact magnitude of these effects — our sample is small.
2. Whether relationships are stable over time, or whether increasing party fragmentation is changing how economic discontent translates into votes.
3. How the prosperity paradox will play out as Nepal continues to urbanize rapidly.

<PullQuote>
  The economy isn't everything in Nepali elections. But it's more than most political commentators
  give it credit for
</PullQuote>

Carville was right. It's the economy. Even in Nepal.
