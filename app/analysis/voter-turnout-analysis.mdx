# Nepal's Voters Are Disappearing. The Data Shows Where They Went.

## KEY FINDINGS

- Turnout dropped **7.8 percentage points** between 2017 and 2022, the sharpest decline outside the civil war period
- **Urban-rural gap widened** from 8 points to 17 points, creating two different electorates
- **Youth turnout fell 6 points** from 58% in 2017 to 52% in 2022, with 18-25 year olds most disengaged
- Roughly **1.4 million voters** would have shown up if participation had held steady
- Evidence-based reforms could push turnout back to **71-78%**

---

In Humla, a remote district in Karnali Province, **75.8%** of eligible voters showed up in 2022. In Kathmandu-1, the capital's political heart, turnout hit **48.3%**.

That's a **27.5-point gap**. In the same country. On the same day.

<PullQuote>
  Nepal's voter turnout story isn't really one story. It's two — and the second one is winning.
</PullQuote>

Between 2017 and 2022, national turnout fell from **68.8% to 61%**. That 7.8-point drop represents roughly **1.4 million voters** who would have shown up if participation had held steady.

<Callout type="warning">
  Turnout dropped from 68.8% in 2017 to 61.0% in 2022 — the steepest decline in Nepal's democratic
  history outside the civil war period.
</Callout>

The short answer: yes, we should be worried. The long answer involves mountains, migration, generational cynicism, and a political class losing its audience.

---

## Three decades of turnout tell a clear story

Nepal has held seven major national elections since 1991. Turnout has bounced around, but the pattern isn't random.

| Election | Year | Type  | Turnout | Eligible Voters |
| -------- | ---- | ----- | ------- | --------------- |
| House 1  | 1991 | FPTP  | 65.2%   | 11,315,000      |
| House 2  | 1994 | FPTP  | 62.8%   | 12,000,000      |
| House 3  | 1999 | FPTP  | 65.8%   | 13,129,000      |
| CA 1     | 2008 | Mixed | 78.3%   | 17,620,556      |
| CA 2     | 2013 | Mixed | 78.6%   | 12,147,234      |
| House 5  | 2017 | Mixed | 68.8%   | 15,371,000      |
| House 6  | 2022 | Mixed | 61.0%   | 17,988,570      |

The 1990s elections hovered in the mid-60s. Then came the Constituent Assembly elections of 2008 and 2013, and turnout spiked to nearly **78%**.

Those weren't ordinary elections. They were votes on what kind of country Nepal would become. The civil war had just ended. The monarchy had been abolished.

Once the constitution was written, voters started leaving. Nepal went from one of South Asia's higher-turnout democracies to one that sits in the **bottom quartile globally**.

For context: India's 2019 election drew **67.4%**. Sri Lanka's 2020 vote hit **75.9%**. The OECD average is **68%**. Nepal, at 61%, is closer to the United States than to its regional peers.

In a village of 500 people, everyone knows whether you voted. There's social pressure, but also social meaning — the election is a community event.

In Kathmandu, nobody knows and nobody cares. You're anonymous. You've got work. The polling station is inconvenient.

Notice that while 18-25 year olds make up 23% of the electorate, they only account for 19% of actual voters. Meanwhile, voters over 60 are just 9% of the electorate but 10% of those who actually vote.

Qualitative research keeps turning up the same themes. "All parties are the same." "Promises never kept." "My single vote doesn't matter."

Nepal has had four different prime ministers since 2017. Coalition partners swap sides like they're changing seats at a dinner party.

If you're 22 and you've watched this your entire adult life, why would you believe your vote changes anything?

## The gender gap is closing — but not everywhere

The gender gap has been shrinking steadily for three decades:

| Year | Male Turnout | Female Turnout | Gap |
| ---- | ------------ | -------------- | --- |
| 1991 | 67.8%        | 62.4%          | 5.4 |
| 1999 | 68.2%        | 63.1%          | 5.1 |
| 2008 | 79.4%        | 77.1%          | 2.3 |
| 2017 | 69.7%        | 67.8%          | 1.9 |
| 2022 | 62.1%        | 59.8%          | 2.3 |

In 1991, women voted at rates **5.4 points below men**. By 2017, the gap was down to 1.9 points. It ticked back up slightly to 2.3 in 2022.

In the Kathmandu Valley, the gap is just **0.8 points**. In rural hill districts, it's 1.5 points. But in rural plains areas, it's still **4.2 points**.

<Callout type="success">
  The gender gap has shrunk from 5.4 points in 1991 to 2.3 points in 2022 — one of Nepal's genuine
  democratic success stories.
</Callout>

## What drove the 2017-2022 decline?

The 7.8-point decline deserves a proper autopsy. There are four plausible explanations.

**1. Disillusionment:** This is the biggest factor. Pre-election polling found satisfaction with democracy dropped from 58% to 42% since 2017. The share who believed "parties care about people like me" fell from 49% to 31%.

**2. Migration:** The 2021 census found 2.1 million people living outside their birth district. Many are still registered back home but can't easily travel to vote.

**3. Youth disengagement:** Young voters didn't cause the entire decline, but they're a disproportionate part of it.

**4. COVID:** The pandemic might explain a point or two, but it can't carry the weight of a nearly 8-point drop.

<PullQuote>
  The decline was concentrated in urban areas with lots of young people experiencing economic
  stress. Rural, remote, older communities barely budged.
</PullQuote>

A regression model helps sort out the relative contributions:

1. Urban status: -4.2 percentage points
2. Youth population share: -2.8 points per 10% increase
3. Rising poverty: -1.4 points per 5% poverty increase
4. Remoteness: +1.8 points (remote areas declined less)

Translation: the decline was concentrated in urban areas with lots of young people experiencing economic stress.

## What could actually fix this?

There are five evidence-based interventions that could push turnout back toward the high 60s or even the 70s.

**1. Automatic voter registration:** Nepal had 17.9 million registered voters in 2022, but census data suggests 20 million or more were eligible. Automatically registering citizens when they turn 18 could add **3 to 5 points**.

**2. Absentee or mobile voting for migrants:** With 2.1 million internal migrants unable to easily vote, some form of vote-by-mail or vote-anywhere system could recover **2 to 4 points**.

**3. Civic education in schools:** This is a long game. Mandatory civics curricula that emphasize participation could narrow the youth gap by **3 to 6 points** over time.

**4. More polling stations:** Adding more stations, especially in areas with difficult terrain, could add **1 to 2 points**.

**5. Simpler ballot design:** Clearer instructions and visual aids could add **half a point to a point**.

If all five were implemented, turnout could plausibly reach **71 to 78%**. That's a big if — these reforms require political will, and the parties currently in power got there with 61% turnout.

## Should we actually worry?

If people aren't voting because they're satisfied, low turnout is a sign of stability, not crisis. Political scientists call this the "politics of happiness" theory.

Nepal's situation is different. The polling data points to **alienation and cynicism**, not satisfaction and not fear. People _can_ vote. They just don't think it matters.

And there's a representational problem baked in. When young, urban voters check out and older, rural voters stay engaged, the resulting parliament reflects **one Nepal more than the other**.

The people most affected by economic policy, urbanization, and the job market are the least represented in the body that makes those decisions.

<Sidebar>
  The 2022 parliament was elected by the oldest, most rural slice of the population — while the
  people most affected by economic policy decisions stayed home.
</Sidebar>

That's not a recipe for a political system that feels legitimate to its citizens.

## What we know and what we don't

**What the data tells us:**

1. Nepal's turnout decline is real and concentrated among young and urban voters
2. The geographic divide in participation is enormous and growing
3. The gender gap is shrinking — a genuine success story
4. There are concrete, evidence-based reforms that could reverse the trend

**What we don't know:**

1. Whether the decline will continue or stabilize
2. How new parties like the RSP will affect mobilization
3. Whether Nepal's political class will take the hint

<Callout type="warning">
  When 39% of your citizens don't bother to vote, they're telling you something. The question is
  whether anyone in power is listening.
</Callout>

The biggest unknown is whether Nepal's political class will act. Or whether they're too busy forming the next coalition to notice.
